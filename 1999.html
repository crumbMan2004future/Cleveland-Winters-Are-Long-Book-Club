Goodbye, 1900s

The Future Starts at Midnight

As the century takes its final bow, America toasts a new millennium that promises to be faster, smarter and more connected than anyone ever imagined

By the Ledger Staff

Washington — The 20th century has less than a day left to live, and almost nobody is sad to see it go.

President Clinton opened the nation’s official Millennium celebration on the National Mall this morning. The light may be fading on the old century, he told the crowd, but “the Sun is still rising on America.”

The White House Millennium Council gave the weekend a theme that suits the mood from coast to coast: honor the past, imagine the future.

The centerpiece of the weekend is a National Millennium Time Capsule, packed with objects that shaped the American century. Among them: Louis Armstrong’s trumpet, a piece of the Berlin Wall and a transistor, the tiny switch that set off the information age. It won’t be opened for another hundred years.

Here on the North Coast, folks are bundling up for a night by the lake, with one eye on the clock and the other on the furnace. (See Y2K, A3)

And what comes after midnight? Ask around and you’ll hear the same word again and again: connected. Computers in every home. Libraries on every desk. Friends on the other side of the world, a click away. If the 1900s were the century of the car and the airplane, the 2000s look like the century of the network.

“The Sun is still rising on America.”President Clinton, this morning

One hundred years ago tonight, President Clinton noted in remarks this afternoon, sending a text by telegraph took six seconds. Tomorrow, a message can circle the planet before you finish your toast.

Happy New Year, Cleveland. See you on the other side.

Technology

‘Information Superhighway’ Will Bring the World Together

The network once pitched as a global town square is about to reach your living room

When Vice President Al Gore laid out his plan for a Global Information Infrastructure before the world’s telecommunications leaders in Buenos Aires in 1994, he promised it would help educate children, let people trade ideas across borders and open up a worldwide marketplace.

He went further. The network, he said, would spread participatory democracy around the globe.

“I see a new Athenian Age of democracy forged in the fora the GII will create.”Vice President Gore, 1994

Five and a half years later, the World Wide Web is everywhere you look: on billboards, on cereal boxes, at the end of the evening news. Tonight, millions of people will share the arrival of 2000 with strangers in chat rooms around the world. The town square has gone global.

The Next Ten Years

Your Life in 2009

Talking computers, smart clothes and a gadget that does everything: two of the decade’s big thinkers sketch what’s next

Inventor Ray Kurzweil’s new book, The Age of Spiritual Machines, takes a crack at 2009. His picture: computers so small they disappear into our clothing and jewelry, keyboards giving way to conversation as we simply talk to our machines, and screens built right into eyeglasses. Pocket-sized machines will read printed pages aloud to blind readers, he writes, while “listening machines” turn speech into text for the deaf.

Microsoft’s Bill Gates, in his own 1999 book, Business @ the Speed of Thought, imagines people carrying small devices that keep them in touch wherever they go, checking the news, their flights and the markets on the move.

He also predicts services that compare prices across many websites at once, private websites where families and friends chat and plan get-togethers, and video feeds that let you check on your house while you’re away.

Cars that drive themselves? Ask us again in 2010.
